## 1. Book architecture

Use this default order:

1. source scope and integrity notice;
2. operational cheatsheet with links;
3. 7–14 day study route;
4. self-contained foundations and required proofs;
5. problem-family method chapters;
6. all historical problems;
7. answer-free mock papers;
8. mock solutions;
9. source and correction index.

Adapt the order only when the course structure requires it.

## 2. Cheatsheet

Write a small, dense tool sheet that can stand on its own during an open-book exam. Include:

- exact definitions and hypotheses;
- theorem conclusions and applicability conditions;
- formulas and standard constructions;
- decision rules for common task types;
- counterexample patterns and common traps;
- concise links to expanded explanations.

A link is navigation, not content. If a reader must follow every link to discover what a theorem
says, the cheatsheet has failed.

## 3. Self-contained foundations

Teach every prerequisite actually used by historical or mock problems. For each nontrivial idea:

1. explain the objects and notation;
2. give an intuitive model without replacing the definition;
3. state the result precisely;
4. explain why its hypotheses matter;
5. work a minimal example or counterexample;
6. prove it fully when the exam expects the proof;
7. show how it enters later problem families.

Do not write a textbook index disguised as notes. A zero-background reader should not need to ask
another source to fill routine logical gaps.

## 4. Problem-family methods

Precede each coherent family with a short method overview:

- recognition cues in the statement;
- the invariant, theorem, normal form, or construction to try;
- a decision tree for competing methods;
- standard proof skeleton;
- boundary cases and common losing moves.

For heterogeneous sections, place a shared strategy map at chapter level rather than forcing
artificial subfamilies.

## 5. Per-problem solution contract

### Statement

Preserve hypotheses, requested conclusions, subproblem structure, scores, and figures. Mark
editorial repairs separately from the source wording.

### Exam-ready formal solution

Write what a student can submit for full credit:

- define every introduced symbol and construction;
- state invoked results with enough hypotheses to justify use;
- close every implication and both directions;
- show calculations and boundary cases needed for grading;
- separate real phases, cases, or subquestions visibly;
- remove coaching commentary, rhetorical questions, and discovery narration.

A short solution may be one continuous proof. A long solution may use natural paragraphs,
`examsteps`, cases, or subheadings. Choose the structure from the proof, not from a template.

### Ideas and motivation

Explain how to rebuild the answer without memorizing it:

- translate the request into a recognizable mathematical structure;
- identify clues that suggest the selected tool;
- explain why tempting alternatives are inefficient or fail when useful;
- derive the key construction or substitution;
- narrate the decisive turn and how later steps follow;
- include intuition, diagnostic checks, and likely exam errors;
- link to exact foundation anchors.

This section is not a hint list or a paraphrase of the formal solution. Use continuous prose when
the reasoning is continuous; use varying numbers of points only for genuinely separate stages.

### Answer-source policy

Prefer a correct official method. Verify it independently, preserve its useful core, and expand it
to the formal and motivational standards above. If no answer exists, author one and record that
provenance. If an official answer is wrong, state and repair the error.

## 6. Mock exams

Infer duration, total score, topic balance, difficulty ramp, and task mix from historical evidence.
For each required mock:

1. provide a standalone answer-free paper with only administrative information, scores, necessary
   assumptions, statements, and figures;
2. provide adequate writing space or a clean print layout;
3. repeat each statement in the later solution section;
4. supply the same formal-solution and motivation standard as for historical problems.

Use one statement source for both appearances when practical to prevent drift.

## 7. Semantic review

Review manually, problem by problem:

- mathematical correctness and source fidelity;
- formal completeness and exam suitability;
- reconstructive quality of motivation;
- natural paragraphing and step counts;
- prerequisite coverage and working links;
- figure sufficiency;
- faithful handling of missing or false sources.

Length thresholds, box counts, and presence of an enumeration are only triage signals.
